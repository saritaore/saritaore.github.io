Job Market Paper

  1. Incentives, Management, and Public Service Delivery: Evidence from a large field experiment in Peru

    Governments often struggle to improve service quality when critical dimensions of effort are difficult to observe. In decentralized systems, central authorities can verify service provision but not the quality of frontline interactions, creating a classic asymmetric information problem. This paper examines whether incentive design can address this challenge by strengthening management practices. I study the effects of two institutional incentive schemes on the implementation and effectiveness of a municipal service delivery intervention aimed at reducing childhood anemia in Peru. Specifically, I compare a standard input-based contract with a mixed incentive contract that conditions rewards on both inputs and outcomes. Leveraging a clustered randomized controlled trial across 539 rural municipalities, I estimate the impact of these contracts on managerial practices, health service utilization, and child health outcomes. Municipalities assigned to the mixed contract increased managerial intensity: they strengthened monitoring, adopted new coordination practices with health facilities, and more actively motivated frontline workers. These changes led to higher utilization of preventive health services, increased uptake of iron supplementation, and greater engagement with the health system. However, despite these improvements in implementation, the intervention did not generate short-run reductions in childhood anemia.

    Presented at: Workshop El mundo rural en América Latina y el Caribe - CAF (2025), LACEA (2025), APPAM (2025), NEUDC (2025), EHEW (2026), AFE (2026), LACEA (2026).
    Funded by: CAF, The Weiss Fund, JPAL Governance Initiative, Columbia University.

Working Paper

  1. Gender-Based Violence in Schools and Girls’ Education: Experimental Evidence from Mozambique
    With Sofia Amaral , Aixa Garcia , Selim Gulesci , Alejandra Ramos , Micaela Sviatschi
    Status: Submitted, under review.

    School-related gender-based violence (GBV) is pervasive, yet evidence on effective interventions to curb violence is limited, particularly in settings where perpetrators face weak accountability and victims and bystanders have limited agency for reporting. We evaluate a policy in Mozambique that combined teacher training to strengthen schools’ capacity to respond to GBV with guided student discussion sessions to increase awareness around violence and reporting of abuse. To examine differential effects and complementarities across genders, student training was randomly targeted to girls, boys, or both. The intervention reduced sexual violence perpetrated by teachers against girls by 67 percent in all treated schools, regardless of the gender of students trained. Yet when girls received the student training, girls’ school enrollment increased by 10 percent. The evidence is consistent with victim engagement playing a role in translating reductions in violence into educational gains: training girls increased reporting of GBV to trained school staff, who were more likely to formally report cases to school authorities. Administrative data from the national child helpline further show increases in GBV-related calls and investigations after our intervention. Our findings highlight the importance of institutional accountability in constraining abuse by powerful actors and of engaging victims in reporting mechanisms to translate safer school environments into human capital investment.

    Media/Policy Mentions: VoxDev, VoxUNICEFDev, Development Impact WB Blog.

  2. Spillover Effects of School Infrastructure Upgrade: Evidence from Peru
    With Fabiola Alba-Vivar

    This paper examines a school infrastructure upgrading program and its direct and indirect impacts on enrollment, school dropout rates, and student achievement. We exploit the program’s rollout over four years before an abrupt redesign left some schools without the upgrade. Our findings suggest that schools receiving the upgrades experienced a 0.58 percentage point reduction in dropout rates. However, we observe spillover effects on nearby schools: an increase in private school closures and a decline in enrollment, predominantly affecting the public sector. Notably, reading test scores in nearby private and public schools also dropped, suggesting that the students leaving these schools were likely those with higher human capital.

    Media/Policy Mentions: Podcast Perú Debate.
    Presented at: Conferencia Internacional de Desarrollo Económico at PUCP (2023), Crime and Education Labs Seminar at University of Chicago (2025).
    Funded by: Consortium for Economic and Social Research.

Advanced Projects

  1. Are Cash Transfer Conditionalities Still Important? Evidence from Colombia on Health Outcomes
    With Bridget Hoffmann , Diego Vera
    Status: Draft coming soon.

    Conditional cash transfer programs tie payments to families’ use of preventive health care, but enforcing these conditions is costly. After decades of such programs, are the conditions still needed, or can habits sustain the behavior they were designed to encourage? We study Colombia’s flagship CCT, Familias en Acción, using an unexpected natural experiment: the suspension of the program’s health conditionality during the COVID-19 pandemic and its later reinstatement. We use nationwide administrative data that track every visit to the health system from 2019 to 2023, linked to household socioeconomic records and social program payments.

  2. Urban Crime and Early Health Investments: Evidence from Peru
    With Isabel Hincapie , Daniela Paz
    Status: Pilot underway (Oct. 2026 - Jan 2027).

    This project examines how unsafe public spaces affect early-childhood human capital. Preventive care for infants depends on repeated trips to health facilities, which exposes caregivers to the risks of the street. We test whether insecurity reduces this investment. In collaboration with the Government of Peru, we randomize a police reform across police precincts in the country’s high-crime areas, reorganizing patrol deployment around high-traffic sites, including health centers. We combine georeferenced crime records with administrative health records to measure infants’ preventive care use.

  3. Staying in School: A Large-Scale RCT on Behavioral Interventions to Reduce Crime and Dropout in Peru
    With Natalia Guerrero , Micaela Sviatschi
    Status: Intervention design.

    Criminal organizations depend on a steady supply of young recruits, and schools have become central recruitment sites. Adolescents who join forgo schooling for short-term payments, misjudging the costs of crime and the returns to education. We test whether correcting these beliefs raises the cost of recruitment on the labor supply side. We redesign a police-led prevention program in Peru as a school-based intervention, embedded in a national incentive scheme across the highest-crime police precincts. A two-stage cluster-randomized design identifies direct and spillover effects on beliefs, recruitment, dropout, and local crime.

Work in Progress

  1. Can Non-Contributory Pensions Buffer Weather Shocks? Evidence from Peru’s Pension 65 Program
    With Bridget Hoffmann , Diego Vera
    Status: Dataset construction, MoU with Ministry of Social Inclusion in process.

    Extreme weather shocks pose a growing threat to poor rural households, and the elderly are among the most vulnerable. This paper examines whether Pension 65, Peru’s non-contributory pension program, can act as a climate adaptation tool by protecting households against these shocks. We build a novel dataset that links the universe of eligible beneficiaries and non-beneficiaries to population censuses, household surveys, and administrative records. Combining this data with high-resolution daily weather data and regression discontinuities in program eligibility, we estimate how the pension shapes the effects of weather shocks on labor supply, consumption, health, and mortality, including spillovers to other household members.

  2. Decentralizing Mental Health Care: The Effects of Community Mental Health Centers in Peru
    With Alessandra Hidalgo
    Status: Dataset construction.

    In low- and middle-income countries, most people with mental disorders go untreated, and those who are treated rely on a few distant psychiatric hospitals. Over the past decade, Peru has tried to close this gap by building a national network of Community Mental Health Centers that provide care at the local level. This paper evaluates that expansion using the staggered opening of centers across the country in a difference-in-differences design. The analysis combines administrative records on health service use, public spending, and facility characteristics with nationally representative household surveys.

  3. Targeting Shortages: The Effects of Compulsory Rural Service on Maternal and Infant Health in Peru
    With Mariel Bedoya
    Status: Data analysis.

    This project examines whether temporary work assignments to poor rural areas expand the provision of preventive healthcare. Using administrative health records from Peru, we study how the arrival of health workers affects the delivery of preventive services.

  4. Incentives, Externalities, and Health: Evidence from Performance Transfers in Peru’s Sanitation Sector
    With Luigi Caloi
    Status: Data construction.

    Central governments increasingly use performance-based transfers to improve local service delivery. But when neighbors matter for the production of a public good, these incentives may not work as intended. How should performance transfers be designed when local governments’ actions generate externalities across jurisdictions? We study this question using performance-based transfers for sanitation services in Peruvian municipalities.
